Breakthroughs

A history of pioneering research

Since its founding in 1890, UChicago scholars have made research discoveries that have transformed our world. From fields ranging from astronomy to immunology and economics to sociology, members of the UChicago community have changed the way we live and think. Trace the history of their breakthrough research throughout the years in the timeline below.




1892

Established first sociology department in the U.S.

UChicago’s Sociology Department was the first in the U.S. Its graduates and faculty have shaped sociological subfields and originated methodologies widely used today.

1896

Introduced innovative ideas in education reform

Philosopher and educator John Dewey opened an elementary school in 1896, embarking on one of the most important educational experiments of the century. That small school became the University of Chicago Laboratory Schools, one of the preeminent pre-collegiate academic institutions in the world. 

Learn more

1899

Discovered ecological succession

Botanist Henry Chandler Cowles pioneered the concept of ecological succession as he studied plant communities along the shores of Lake Michigan in the nearby Indiana Dunes. He is considered one of the founders of modern ecology.

Learn more

1900

Helped create field of social psychology

Prof. George Herbert Mead is regarded as one of the founders of the American sociological tradition, particularly the school known as Symbolic Interactionism, as well as the philosophical paradigm known as Pragmatism. His work includes theories of the mind and how the human self develops through social interaction.

1907

Measured the speed of light

Prof. Albert A. Michelson made groundbreaking measurements of the speed of light—providing support for Einstein’s theory of relativity. In 1907, Michelson became the first scientist from the United States to win a Nobel Prize.

Learn more

1908

Pioneered social service scholarship in the U.S.

The School of Social Service Administration (now known as the Crown Family School of Social Work, Policy and Practice) was among the first schools of social work in the U.S. and a pioneer in using scientific research to identify and address the root causes of social inequity. Today, Crown Family School alumni lead public and private welfare agencies around the world.

Learn more

1916

Reconceptualized history of Western civilization

Prof. James Henry Breasted argued that the roots of Western civilization lay in the Middle East, rather than Greek and Roman cultures. He coined the term 'Fertile Crescent' for the area where Mesopotamian cultures flourished millennia ago.

Photo via Hanna Holborn Gray Special Collections Research Center

Learn more

1917

Introduced blood preservation and blood banks

Oswald Robertson discovered a way to preserve blood and pioneered the idea of blood banks, which he established during World War I with the U.S. Army Medical Corps.

1920

Ushered in modern empirical sociology

UChicago sociologists William I. Thomas and Florian Witold Znaniecki won international acclaim as co-authors of The Polish Peasant in Europe and America (1918–20), today considered the foundation of modern empirical sociology.

1921

Created cultural encyclopedia of ancient Mesopotamia

The Chicago Assyrian Dictionary, a 90-year project begun in 1921 and completed in 2011, collected a wealth of information about ancient Near Eastern civilization. In essence a cultural encyclopedia of ancient Mesopotamian history, it is one of the most important contributions to knowledge of ancient Near Eastern civilizations.

Photo by Jason Smith

Learn more

1921

Reconceptualized the study of politics as a science

In 1921, political scientist Charles Merriam outlined an alternate vision for political science, founded on behavioralism. This became the Chicago School of Political Science, which reconceived the study of politics as a scientific endeavor on the model of the natural sciences.

1923

Discovered use of ethylene gas as an anesthetic

In 1923, UChicago physiologist Arno B. Luckhardt discovered the anesthetic use of ethylene gas. In the 20 years following his discovery, ethylene came into general use as an anesthetic in major operations.

1924

Launched the Epigraphic Survey

Prof. James Henry Breasted developed a technique to record the historic texts carved into temples and tombs without damaging them. Today known as the Chicago House Method, the process blends photography, illustration and careful eyes to create an exact copy. For over 100 years, the Epigraphic Survey team has traveled to Luxor, Egypt each fall to precisely document this ancient human history.

Learn more

1928

Inspired the field of urban sociology

Prof. Louis Wirth proposed a new academic paradigm for city life as a sociological construct, which was central to the establishment of urban sociology as a field of study. Wirth’s contributions, along with groundbreaking research by sociologists Ernest Burgess and Robert Park, provided the foundation for the Chicago School of Sociology.

1930

Transformed the field of gastroenterology

Prof. Joseph B. Kirsner pioneered modern understanding and treatment of inflammatory bowel disease and was one of the first to show the link between ulcerative colitis and increased risk of colon cancer. Kirsner also introduced revolutionary guidelines for how physicians should care for patients.

 

1930

Developed our understanding of molecular structure

Prof. Robert S. Mulliken developed molecular orbit theory, which showed how to compute the structure of molecules. He would receive the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1966.

1930

Proposed the existence of black holes

Prof. Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar was the first to propose the existence of black holes and to discover the maximum mass of a white dwarf star. Chandrasekhar won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1983 for these and other contributions that are now central to the field of astrophysics.

Learn more

1931

Discovered cosmic rays are charged particles

Prof. Arthur Compton organized a global survey of cosmic rays (a term coined by Prof. Robert A. Millikan), incoming particles from space, and discovered that these particles are charged. UChicago scientists made many seminal cosmic ray discoveries, such as Prof. John A. Simpson’s invention of detectors which are still used today to monitor incoming space radiation.

Photo by Chicago Sun/Hanna Holborn Gray Special Collections Research Center

Learn more

1937

Established transaction cost economics

Economist and Nobel laureate Ronald Coase offered groundbreaking insights about why firms exist and established the field of transaction cost economics. He also published the Coase Theorem, which holds that under conditions of perfect competition, private and social costs are equal. Economists have applied this theory to virtually every area of human activity.

Learn more

1941

Introduced hormonal treatment of cancer

In 1941, Prof. Charles B. Huggins treated patients suffering from advanced prostate cancer by removing the hormone the cancers needed to grow—a breakthrough for which he would later receive the Nobel Prize. Hormonal treatment has since become a mainstay of care for several types of cancer, including breast and gynecological cancers.

1942

Conducted the first controlled, self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction

The modern nuclear age began on Dec. 2, 1942, when Nobel laureate Prof. Enrico Fermi and his colleagues conducted the first controlled, self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction, on the University of Chicago campus.

Learn more

1946

Determined that fluoride reduces cavities

Dental researcher J. Roy Blayney conducted a 15-year public health experiment in the Chicago suburbs of Oak Park and Evanston that demonstrated the cavity-fighting properties of fluoride in drinking water. The study led to widespread fluoridation of municipal water supplies.

1947

Influenced how slavery is studied and taught

Historian John Hope Franklin, best known for From Slavery to Freedom: A History of African Americans (1947), influenced the way American history is studied and taught. His work aimed to accurately represent African Americans in American history to ensure “the story of the United States could be told adequately and fairly.” His landmark three-part 1976 lecture series focused on the history of race in the United States from the revolution to the 20th century.

Learn more

1949

Performed the first bone marrow transplant

Prof. Leon O. Jacobson performed the first bone marrow transplant. The procedure now helps thousands of patients with cancer and other diseases each year.

1952

Showed how life could have begun on Earth

Two UChicago scientists showed they could create a chemical “primordial soup” that spontaneously formed amino acids—the building blocks of proteins—when they passed electricity through it. The Miller-Urey experiment is famous for suggesting how life could have begun on Earth.

Learn more

Photo by Frank Fritz/Hanna Holborn Gray Special Collections Research Center

1952

Discovered the basic mechanism in chemical reactions

Prof. Henry Taube related the rates of chemical reactions to electronic structure and showed how molecules build a chemical bridge to transfer electrons, rather than simply exchanging electrons, as previously thought. Taube received the 1983 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.

1953

Discovered REM sleep

UChicago opened the world’s first sleep laboratory in 1925. Decades later, two UChicago scientists revolutionized sleep research when they announced the discovery of rapid eye movement (REM) sleep and its association with dreaming.

Photo by Bruce Richardson/Hanna Holborn Gray Special Collections Research Center

Learn more

1956

Revolutionized legal education and services

Founded in 1956, the Mandel Legal Aid Clinic was one of the first of its kind. Through the clinic, students gain hands-on experience and provide pro bono legal aid to low-income residents. It revolutionized both how law students gained practical skills and how high-quality legal services were provided to clients.

Learn more

1957

Established the field of action anthropology

Prof. Sol Tax helped establish the field of action anthropology, an approach in which the researcher has two coordinate goals: to help a group of people in the culture being studied solve a problem and to learn in the process.

 

1958

Discovered the solar wind

Prof. Eugene Parker showed that there is a flow of supersonic particles coming off the surface of the sun, which shapes our entire solar system. For this and other pioneering work, Parker became the first living person to have a spacecraft named after them: the Parker Solar Probe, launched in 2018.

1959

Changed understanding of the jury system

The Chicago Jury Project, the 1950s effort of law professors Harry Kalven and Hans Zeisel, was the first large-scale empirical study of the jury system, revolutionizing the use of social science techniques in legal scholarship. The project changed the American understanding of the role of the jury.

1960

Invented carbon-14 dating

The discovery that ancient organic materials could be dated based on the abundance of an isotope of carbon—called carbon-14 dating—has had revolutionary implications for archaeology and paleontology. Prof. Willard F. Libby won the Nobel Prize for this work in 1960.

Learn more

1965

Discovered where meteorites come from

Prof. Edward Anders discovered that meteorites come from asteroids—not from the moon or other debris—by analyzing the ratios of element isotopes.

Learn more

1965

Discovered proinsulin, advancing diabetes treatment

Biochemist Donald F. Steiner, SM’56, MD’56, discovered proinsulin, the first “pro-hormone” and precursor to insulin. The 1965 finding led to the synthetic production of human insulin, markedly improving therapy for diabetes sufferers, and laid the groundwork for improved understanding of how other proteins in the body are made.

1965

Developed efficient market hypothesis

The efficient market hypothesis posits that prices in the market reflect all available information, as rational investors seek and quickly respond to that information. This idea, the brainchild of economist and Nobel laureate Eugene Fama, has contributed greatly to the creation of indexed funds as an effective strategy of investment.

Learn more

1971

Devised the standard for measuring tornado severity

Prof. Tetsuya “Ted” Fujita developed the Fujita Tornado Scale, or F-scale, by cataloging the damage left in the wake of a tornado. A modified version remains the internationally accepted standard for measuring tornado severity.

Learn more

 

1973

Developed predictor of the value of derivatives

Profs. Myron Scholes and Fischer Black developed an equation that could predict the value of derivatives. The Black-Scholes options pricing model, published in 1973, enabled the growth of futures and options markets and remains key to modern investment strategy.

1975

Confirmed cancer is genetic disease

Prof. Janet Davison Rowley identified the genetic basis for leukemia, confirming that cancer is a genetic disease. She later was awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom and was a key player in the development of the first precisely targeted anti-cancer drug.

Learn more

1976

Introduced the modern monetarist school of economics

Economist Milton Friedman’s work led to monetarism, a radical rethinking of the sources of inflation that overturned conventional Keynesian thinking. He argued that the money supply, not government spending, influenced output and the price level, which strongly influenced Federal Reserve policy. He won a Nobel Prize in 1976.

1977

Discovered source of red blood cell formation

Prof. Eugene Goldwasser isolated erythropoietin, the hormone behind red blood cell formation. The discovery led to the development of the first blockbuster drug of the biotech age, which has since treated millions with anemia.

1978

Identified earliest evidence of hominins walking upright

Prof. Russell Tuttle, a leading expert on early humans, analyzed 3.66-million-year-old footprints discovered in Laetoli, Tanzania. He determined that they were left by creatures who walked bipedally in a fashion almost identical to human beings. These “Laetoli footprints” were the oldest known evidence at the time of early hominins walking upright.

1983

Instituted breakthrough math curricula

With the goal of improving mathematics education in elementary and secondary schools throughout the U.S., the UChicago School Mathematics Project was founded in 1983. An estimated 3.5 to 4 million students now use the project’s materials, such as Everyday Mathematics.

1983

Laid the groundwork for modern understanding of banking and bank runs

Prof. Douglas Diamond co-wrote a seminal paper outlining the role of banking and financial institutes in society, and why bank runs happen. The theory transformed the way people think about banking and laid the groundwork for how central bankers, regulators, policymakers and academics approach modern finance.

Learn more

1984

Pioneered clinical medical ethics

Physician Mark Siegler began teaching clinical medical ethics at the University of Chicago in the late 1970s. The MacLean Center for Clinical Medical Ethics, founded in 1984 as the first academic institute dedicated to clinical medical ethics, supports the study of ethical issues raised during everyday care. Its work aims to improve physicians’ skills and performance in decision-making and caring for patients.

1988

Laid foundations for modern solar energy tech

Prof. Roland Winston invented devices that can concentrate sunlight to more than 60,000 times its normal intensity—which today underlie many modern solar energy technologies.

Learn more

1990

Discovered previously unknown dinosaur species

Beginning in the 1990s, paleontologist Paul Sereno’s discoveries of previously unknown dinosaur species on several continents have contributed to the understanding of the dinosaur family tree and to the larger question of how evolution works over millions of years.

1992

Opened the social sciences to economic analysis

Many current economists follow Nobel laureate Gary Becker in exploring aspects of modern life through the lens of markets and incentives. Becker expanded the scope of inquiry in economics to include human capital, the family, crime, discrimination, and other topics previously in the domain of sociology or psychology—winning a Nobel Prize in 1992.

Learn more

 

1992

Shaped our understanding of early childhood classrooms

Early childhood education pioneer and UChicago Lab Schools teacher Vivian Gussin Paley published her acclaimed book You Can’t Say You Can’t Play in 1992. Lessons from the book, which looks at the social and moral landscape of the classroom, have helped shape the educational approach at many schools.

 

1993

Changed tools used to study historical economies

Nobel laureate Prof. Robert Fogel shifted the course of how economic historians analyze past economies, using the theoretical tools and quantitative methods rigorously developed to answer modern economic questions as a lens through which to view the past. This radically changed the insights scholars could glean from historical data.

Learn more

1994

Helped discover top quark

Scientists at Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory announced the first direct experimental evidence for the top quark—the last undiscovered quark of the six predicted to exist by current scientific theory. Many UChicago scientists collaborated on the discovery, including Prof. Mel Shochet, who served as scientific co-spokesperson for the collaboration.

Photo via Fermilab

 

1994

Promoted the study of contemporary Chinese art in the West

Art historian Prof. Wu Hung is widely considered the first person to introduce contemporary Chinese art to the West and open it as a field of critical study. Wu’s work exposed Western audiences to a deeper understanding of Chinese art at a time of great economic and social change.

 

1998

Coined term ‘dark energy’

After scientists discovered evidence that the universe is expanding faster and faster all the time, Prof. Michael Turner coined the term ‘dark energy’ to refer to what was causing the expansion. The nature of dark energy remains one of the great mysteries of physics.

Illustration via NASA, ESA, CSA, Joseph Olmsted (STScI)

Learn more

 

1999

Invented grid computing

Grid computing, co-invented by computer scientist Prof. Ian Foster in 1999, paved the way for modern cloud computing, allowing large-scale computing services to be delivered reliably and securely on demand.

Learn more

2000

Cofounded the Sloan Digital Sky Survey

UChicago Profs. Don York and Rich Kron were among the founders of the groundbreaking Sloan Digital Sky Survey, which created a well-calibrated map of the universe used for hundreds of thousands of scientific studies ranging from asteroids to the large-scale structure of the universe.

Learn more

2002

Measured cosmic microwave background radiation

Measurements of the cosmic microwave background radiation—the 14-billion-year-old light from just after the Big Bang—by Prof. John Carlstrom verified the framework that supports modern cosmological theory. He led a team that built the South Pole Telescope, which maps the cosmic microwave background from Antarctica.

Learn more

2004

Launched interdisciplinary research into race, politics, and culture

In 2004, Prof. Cathy Cohen began the Black Youth Project, a national research project devoted to examining the attitudes, resources, and culture of African American youth.

Learn more

2005

Advanced clinical electrophysiology

Prof. Harry A. Fozzard pioneered modern understanding of chemical and electrical signaling in heart muscle cells, helping to lay the foundation for modern clinical electrophysiology by mapping out the structure and function of the voltage-gated ion channels in heart muscle.

2005

Began archiving videos of South Side life

Prof. Jacqueline Stewart put out a call for the historical treasure troves hidden in basements and attics—home movies. Today, the South Side Home Movie Project holds over 1,200 reels of footage shot by South Siders from the 1930s to the 1980s, all held in a climate-controlled vault at the Logan Center for the Arts.

Learn more

2006

Discovered evolutionary link between fish and land animals

In 2006, paleontologist Neil Shubin discovered Tiktaalik roseae, a fossil with both fins and the beginnings of feet—from the time that animals began to move from living solely in oceans onto land, 375 million years ago. Shubin’s key discovery advanced evolutionary biology, and his best-selling book and television series sparked popular interest in the subject.

Learn more

2007

Helped lay the foundation of mechanism design theory

Economist Prof. Roger Myerson radically advanced the field of mechanism design, tackling the problem of how to best design markets with the right incentives to induce brokers, investors, and other actors to reveal information to one another truthfully. This led to a branch of scholarship investigating effective trading mechanisms, regulations, and voting procedures, and earned Myerson the 2007 Nobel Prize in Economics.

Learn more

2008

Launched Crime Lab to study and prevent crime

The UChicago Crime Lab partners with policymakers in Chicago and across the country to carry out large-scale policy experiments to identify effective and cost-effective ways to help prevent crime and violence.

Learn more

2009

Identified loneliness as a health risk factor

Having few close friends contributes to poorer health for many older adults, but researchers have found that feeling lonely poses an even greater risk. Prof. Linda Waite found that older adults who feel most isolated report 65% more depressive symptoms than those who feel least isolated, regardless of their actual levels of connectedness.

Learn more

2010

Premiered stage adaptations of classic novels by African Americans

Court Theatre at UChicago was the first to bring Ralph Ellison’s Invisible Man and Richard Wright’s Native Son to the stage. These critically acclaimed productions further solidified Court’s strong reputation for staging compelling African American drama.

2010

Rearranged our picture of how genes work

Biochemist Prof. Chuan He discovered that a previously unknown process called reversible RNA methylation is actually central to how genes are expressed in every single organism. That finding launched a new era of RNA modification research, helping scientists more fully understand everything from circadian rhythms to cancer.

Learn more

2012

Contributed key expertise to the Higgs boson discovery

University of Chicago physicists played a key role in developing electronics that led to the celebrated discovery of the long-sought Higgs boson, the particle that endows all elementary particles in the universe with mass, in 2012.

Learn more

2016

Developed philosophy focused on human capabilities

Prof. Martha Nussbaum received the Kyoto Prize in part for her work toward the development of the Capabilities Approach, which measures human welfare according to the opportunities afforded to citizens of different countries for realizing their potential and flourishing as individuals, rather than using GDP as a proxy.

Learn more

2017

Launched the Center for Contemporary Composition

The Chicago Center for Contemporary Composition is a collaborative space for music writing and performance at the University of Chicago. It is led by Augusta Read Thomas, the University Professor of Composition—a world-renowned, Grammy-winning composer recognized for her eminence in the field of composition.

2017

Helped make the first measurement of ripples in space-time from colliding stars

The LIGO collaboration announced their detectors picked up the extremely faint ripple in space-time, called a gravitational wave, made by two colliding neutron stars more than 100 million years ago. Among other firsts, the signal allowed Prof. Daniel Holz to make the first calculation of how fast the universe is expanding using gravitational waves.

Illustration by R. Hurt/Caltech-JPL

Learn more

2017

Pioneered the field of behavioral economics

Prof. Richard Thaler won the Nobel Prize for scholarship demonstrating that humans are predictably irrational: They don’t always make the ‘right’ choice in a traditional economic sense, instead acting on impulses and incomplete information. Thaler’s work has also shown that because people are predictable, they can be “nudged” to make better choices—a concept popularized in his 2008 book Nudge with then-Law School Professor Cass R. Sunstein.

Learn more

2018

Identified silent film that reframes early cinematic history

The 1898 film Something Good-Negro Kiss is the earliest known cinematic depiction of African American affection. Prof. Allyson Nadia Field worked with its discoverers to identify the film’s origins, tracing it to the Chicago studio of William Selig.

Learn more

2020

Opened an experimental quantum loop

Researchers at UChicago and Argonne National Lab opened a 50+ mile underground “quantum loop” in the Chicago area to serve as a testbed for communications and computing of the future. The project hopes to leverage the principles of quantum physics to send unhackable information across long distances.

Learn more

Photo by Robert Kozloff

2020

Helped world respond to the COVID-19 pandemic

UChicago scientists did essential early work to image and understand the structure of the COVID-19 virus; physicians at University of Chicago Medicine helped pioneer life-saving treatment protocols for COVID-19; and epidemiologists helped advise the state of Illinois, the city of Chicago, and the public worldwide on how to prevent the spread of the virus.

2021

Figured out how to corral molecules into a quantum state

A team led by Prof. Cheng Chin showed how to bring multiple molecules at once into a single quantum state—one of the most important goals in quantum physics. The breakthrough could mean new discoveries in quantum science, including new technology.

Learn more

2022

Helped take first direct images of black holes

The University of Chicago-led South Pole Telescope was part of a network of telescopes called the Event Horizon Telescope that produced the first-ever image of a black hole, and the first image of the supermassive black hole at the center of our own galaxy.

Learn more

2025

Unveiled duck-billed dinosaur ‘mummies’

Researchers discovered how E. annectens dinosaur “mummies” preserved intricate details of skin, spikes, and hooves, thanks to an ultra-thin clay template formed during a rare fossilization event. The team reconstructed the complete fleshy profile of these dinosaurs, revealing new anatomical insights, such as the earliest-known hooves in a land vertebrate.

Learn more
